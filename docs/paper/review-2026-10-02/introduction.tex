\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:introduction}

Continuous payments require incoming value to remain usable across a
sequence of transfers. In a UTXO ledger, making each successor wait for
its source transaction to execute publicly inserts confirmation delays
between otherwise ready payments. Consider Alice paying Bob, who uses
the received output to pay Carol. Bob needs evidence that supports a new
payment before Alice's transaction executes. The difficulty is not only
speed: once Bob's payment executes first, someone must stand behind the
value that Alice's transaction has not yet delivered, and the accounting
must stay correct whichever transaction reaches the ledger first, or
whether Alice's ever does.

Certificate-based payment systems show that recipient-side usability can
be separated from parts of public processing. FastPay and Zef support
low-latency certified payments, while Sui Lutris combines broadcast and
consensus paths for different forms of state
access~\cite{fastpay,zef,lutris}. Payment-channel protocols provide
rapid transfers through channel relationships and their
liquidity~\cite{blitz,donner}. We address a specific native-ledger execution requirement: a certified output whose source has
not yet executed should support a publicly executed successor, and
responsibility for the missing source should fall on an identified party
with funds set aside for it. In Next, users spend their own routed
outputs and registered organizations back unresolved sources, with no
prefunded channel path between counterparties.

Three coupled problems arise. First, certificates can circulate before
public registration, so public gap accounting misses some existing
commitments; signing capacity must cover every certified payment,
including hidden ones. Second, a source may arrive after the committee
has compensated its missing value. The late arrival must not pay the same
value twice, to the guarantor that advanced it and to the recipient who
had already spent it; otherwise delay itself would move funds. Third,
resolving a source may concern a payment whose outputs have already been
consumed, so any change to its recorded funding must preserve the owner
authorization, output identifiers, and commitments that successors
reference. A stable transaction hash alone does not specify or authorize
such a change.

Next fixes payment rights while assigning unresolved sources to their
direct issuers. An output certificate (TXCer) binds a payment's outputs
to an identified issuing organization. After verifying and atomically
recording an output and its TXCer, the recipient can request a successor
through the output's designated consumption organization, carrying direct
input certificates as ancestry evidence; owner authorization, spend
state, fees, and resource limits are checked separately.

If a certified input is still absent from public creation state, the
committee atomically consumes it, records a direct obligation against
the issuer, and creates the child outputs, which are final even while the
obligation is open. Successful execution of the source fulfills the
obligation. After its deadline, public compensation closes it through an
actual reserve debit that supplies the missing backing rather than
crediting the child recipient again. If the source later arrives, it
repays the issuer that advanced the value, in the same transition, and
creates no second output for the recipient. When all sources eventually execute, delay changes temporary capital
exposure while preserving the final principal allocation.

Economic closure admits an append-only formulation. Next couples a
compensation with a validated funding revision in one public transition;
compensation progress therefore also requires three adaptation
contributions. The accounting invariants are enforced independently of
the adapter output. The repair interface installs a restricted funding
replacement under the existing commitments, retaining a historical
representation of the actual reserve debit.
Standard wallets, synchronization, and replay read original commands; the
revised body is read by repair construction, installation, and local
inspection. We organize the design into three contributions with distinct
responsibilities.

\noindent\textbf{C1: A layered architecture for one-round continuation.}
We separate member certification, recipient delivery, evidence
replication, and public execution. Members atomically lock inputs and
reserve capacity before signing, one parallel certification round
produces a TXCer, and installation and submission proceed in the
background. Registered organizations use one evidence interface within
and across organizations, with payment principal separate from guarantor
reserves.

\noindent\textbf{C2: Direct responsibility with funded, delay-neutral
accounting.} We define the atomic missing-input transition and its
normal, compensated, and recovered closure paths; for fixed input and
certificate shapes, per-request ancestry evidence does not grow with
transfer depth. Under the fixed-membership Byzantine model, we prove
unique consumption, that signing capacity bounds the liability of every
certificate ever signed including hidden ones, and asset conservation.
We also prove that, for a finite set of non-conflicting payments whose
sources all execute, any interleaving of arrivals and compensations ends
with the same CAL holdings and reserve balances as undelayed execution.

\noindent\textbf{C3: Identity-preserving authorized representation
updates.} We restrict funding changes to
publicly authorized slots while preserving owner authorizations, output
references, and the complete block identity, including its part-set
header. Same-block batches share final-part adaptation while applying
economic steps in order, and installation advances directly to a
committed revision without repeating economic effects. This mechanism gives repair constructors and installers a commitment-compatible
historical representation with bounded, shared adaptation work.

On a single M4 Max host with nine services, in-memory service state, and
NoSync wallets, 100-hop chains reach their last certified receipt in a
three-run median of 158.846\,ms, versus 65.204\,s with public-confirmation
waits between hops. Three 30,000-payment runs complete 1,750--1,887
payments/s including background drain. A separate nine-run recovery
matrix closes 21,600 payments and repays all 216 compensations; paired
executions preserve the final distribution of CAL. Sections~\ref{sec:model}
and~\ref{sec:protocol} define the design; Sections~\ref{sec:security}
and~\ref{sec:evaluation} develop its safety argument and evaluation.
